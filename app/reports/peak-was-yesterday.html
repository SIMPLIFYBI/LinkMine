Peak Was Yesterday

The world mined more mercury in 1971 than in any year since. More asbestos in 1978. More diamonds in 2006. Mining is supposed to keep growing, but in more than fifty years of records, nine minerals have already peaked and are now mined less. Among them is antimony, a metal on both the US and EU lists of critical minerals.

Most government plans for critical minerals assume the same thing: demand will rise, so supply has to rise with it. For most minerals that holds. Copper, iron ore, gold, bauxite and lithium are all at or near record output.

But look across the full record from the British Geological Survey (BGS), 190 countries from 1970 to 2024, and a different group stands out: minerals whose world peak isn’t a forecast but a date in the past. Some were banned or restricted. One is shrinking because of the very scarcity that makes it strategic.

The one that should worry people

Antimony is on the United States critical minerals list and the European Union's critical raw materials list. It hardens lead, goes into flame retardants and sits in every lead-acid battery. World mine production peaked in 2013 and is now at 60% of that level: a 40% fall over a decade in which demand did not fall at all. Nobody banned it and nothing replaced it. Deposits are running down and exports are restricted. Unlike mercury or asbestos, nobody chose this.

How this was built, and what it can't tell you

The source. BGS World Mineral Statistics: 410,021 country-year records of production, imports and exports, 1970 to 2024, 190 countries, 521 commodity codes. BGS has compiled these figures since 1913; everything before 1970 exists only on paper. Used here with the permission of the British Geological Survey, whose published terms otherwise limit reuse to non-commercial research.

World totals are summed, not published

BGS publishes no world row, so each total here is the sum of the countries reporting that mineral that year. That makes coverage the main risk, and it is the reason four candidate minerals were thrown out rather than published:

  • Cement — BGS's reporters account for about 7% of real world output. The apparent trend is a gap in coverage, not a real one.
  • Rare earth minerals — fall to 0.2% of peak with a single reporter left. That is a reclassification, not a collapse; rare earth output has grown enormously.
  • Mica — jumps from 59 to 100 and back to 66 across 2012–16. A reporting discontinuity, not a peak.
  • Primary aggregates — BGS reports them as one term spanning crushed stone and sand and gravel, so no single commodity figure is valid.

Cross-checked against a second publisher

For 2024, BGS country sums were compared against the USGS published world totals. Where both cover the same material on the same basis they agree closely — gold 101.6%, crude steel 99.6%, mine lead 100.3%, primary aluminium 100.8%, alumina 100.9%, bauxite 96%. That agreement is why I trust the earlier years, where no second source exists.

Why three-year averages

Single-year peaks are usually reporting quirks. Talc appeared to peak in 1997 at a level 29% above the year before and 40% above the year after; smoothed, its real plateau is 1990. Graphite appeared to peak in 1995 on a one-year doubling in a single country's reported figure; smoothed, its peak is 2008. Every figure here is a three-year centred average.

One reading to resist

A falling producer count is not proof of a falling world total — it can equally mean the data got thinner. So the direction of coverage is published in the table beside every figure. For diamond and platinum-group metals, more countries report now than at peak, which makes those declines hard to explain away. For asbestos the producer count collapsed from 23 to 4, and there the shrinking list is the finding: countries stopped producing because they banned the material.

What a zero means

BGS states the precision of every figure, which most publishers leave you to infer. Of 410,021 records, 6,229 say -----, meaning nothing was produced; 3,327 say ..., meaning the figure is unknown and stored as null rather than zero; and 624 carry a printed 0 that BGS explicitly states is real output between 0 and 0.5 units. Those 624 are not zero production, and treating them as such would be the easiest mistake in this dataset. None of them are counted as zero here.

Still true, and not claimed

Past peak is not the same as running out, and it is not a reserve statement. Mercury and asbestos fell because the world decided it wanted less of them — that is policy succeeding, not scarcity. Diamond's fall owes much to one mine closing, Argyle in Western Australia, plus laboratory stones taking share. And a mineral can turn over again: antimony's own 2013 peak came after an earlier high in 2006.
