The Mine-Dependent Six

Ask which countries depend on mining and you get a long list. Ask two World Bank measures to agree, in every period we tested, and the list is six: Mongolia, DR Congo, Zambia, Papua New Guinea, Chile and Peru. Two more, Mali and Burkina Faso, earn mineral rents on a par with the six, but only one measure can see it.

Both measures are blunt. One is trade: ores and metals as a share of what a country sells abroad. The other is mineral rents, which is what mining earns after costs, as a share of the whole economy. A country can look dependent on one and not the other, and the tables below show many that do. The six here clear both bars in every period tested: ores and metals at least a quarter of goods exports, and mineral rents at least 3% of GDP.

The two gold economies are the other half of the story. The World Bank's trade measure leaves gold out, so Mali and Burkina Faso, which earn 6.8% and 5.3% of GDP in mineral rents on average, show ores and metals at under 2% of their exports. In our BGS data Mali mined 73 tonnes of gold in 2023 and Burkina Faso 57.

How this was built, and what it can't tell you

The sources. Two World Bank World Development Indicators: Mineral rents (% of GDP) and Ores and metals exports (% of merchandise exports), for countries only (World Bank aggregates are excluded). Rents cover 2015–21 for 197 countries; exports run to 2023 for 163. Gold figures are from BGS World Mineral Statistics.

The rule

  • The six: mineral rents of at least 3% of GDP in each of three windows (2015–21, 2015–19, 2017–21) and ores and metals of at least 25% of goods exports in both 2021–23 and 2015–19.
  • The two gold economies: the same rents test, and ores and metals under 5% of exports in both periods.
  • No composite score. The two measures are never added or averaged, because they measure different things.

The thresholds are judgments, and they are stated so a reader can move them. Three windows are used for rents because the 2021 boom would otherwise decide the answer. Raise the rents line to 4% and Chile (3.4% in 2015–19) and Peru (3.9%) fall below it in the earlier window, though both stay far above the line on exports. Australia, Tajikistan and Mauritania miss the 3% line in 2015–19 (2.8%, 2.9% and 2.5%), so they are not in the six. The table shows every miss.

What the trade measure counts

By the World Bank's definition the category covers ores, concentrates and non-ferrous metals. It includes refined metal, so economies built on smelting appear alongside miners, and it excludes gold. Several countries in the trade-only table are known for aluminium smelting or processing, such as Iceland, Bahrain and Montenegro. That is background knowledge from outside this data, not something the figures show. The measure is also volatile: Panama went from 10.6% in 2015–19 to 78.5% in 2021–23.

What rents count

Mineral rents are the value of mineral production at world prices less the cost of producing it, as a share of GDP, for the World Bank's list of ten minerals: tin, gold, lead, zinc, iron ore, copper, nickel, silver, bauxite and phosphate. Diamonds, cobalt, uranium and lithium are not on the list, and coal, oil and gas are reported separately, so economies that depend on those are undercounted here. Rents say nothing about who owns the mines, or how much of the income stays in the country.

Still not claimed

This is dependence on mining as a whole, not on one commodity, and it is not a measure of how much a country benefits. The data is two to four years old.
