The World’s Biggest Bauxite Miner Barely Refines It

Guinea mined a third of the world’s bauxite in 2024. It made just 0.25% of the world’s alumina, the next step on the way to aluminium. By the time you get to the metal, the country at the top of the list is China.

Think of an aluminium can. Before there was metal to roll into a sheet, there was a reddish rock called bauxite. A refinery turns the rock into alumina, a white powder. A smelter uses electricity to turn the powder into new metal. Each step can happen in a different country.

Guinea’s split was more than a curiosity in a table. In October 2024, its customs authorities halted bauxite exports from one operator’s mine near Boké. The USGS identifies a dispute over a promised local alumina refinery as a major reason. A country that already led the world in mining the rock wanted more of the processing to happen at home.

Follow the rock to the metal

Australia starts ahead of China at the mine and stays a major refiner. Its share drops to 2% at the smelter. China goes the other way, from 19% of the ore to around 60% of both the powder and the metal. If you stopped at the mine, you’d miss where the supply chain is most concentrated.

The map shows where production happened. Individual shipments, ownership and how much of the value stays in the country are separate questions. Recycled aluminium sits outside the primary-metal figures.

The processing map got more concentrated

At the start of the century, the three biggest smelting countries made about 40% of the primary aluminium in the BGS figures. By 2022–24, the top three made 71%. Refining was even more concentrated, with almost four fifths of reported alumina output coming from just three countries.

Mining concentrated too, but the biggest increases happened in different places. Guinea added about 105 million tonnes a year of bauxite between the two periods. China added about 78 million tonnes a year of alumina and 38 million tonnes of primary aluminium. Each was the largest increase at its stage among countries with figures in all six years.

Guinea accounted for about 41% of the increase in reported bauxite output. China accounted for about 89% of the increase in alumina output. That helps explain why looking at the mine gives such a different picture from looking at the refinery. Shares can move when countries appear or disappear from the figures. I repeated the comparison using just the countries with figures in all six years, and the top three gained share at every stage even there.

Trade links the two stories. China was the main destination for Guinea’s bauxite and alumina exports, according to the USGS. How I measured it

I used the 2024 country figures from the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026, as loaded in YouMine. Each share divides a country’s output by the USGS world total for that stage, including the USGS estimate for countries it groups together. The world totals are rounded, and some country figures are estimates.

The three stages have different products and denominators. A tonne of bauxite is a tonne of ore, while a tonne of aluminium is a tonne of metal. The shares compare the geography of production at each step.

For the timeline, I used the British Geological Survey’s annual bauxite, alumina and primary aluminium series. Each share is relative to the total of the country figures available for that stage and year. Reporting coverage changes, and some estimates differ from the USGS snapshot. Figures for the former Serbia and Montenegro count toward the historical totals and can be viewed in the country selector.

For the two-period comparison, I averaged the three annual BGS totals in each window. A country’s share is its average output divided by that period’s reported total. I ranked the countries by those averages and added the first three shares. The growth table names the largest increase among countries with all six annual figures and compares it with the change across everyone who reported. Unreported values stay blank in the map. They contribute no amount to the totals of reported output.
