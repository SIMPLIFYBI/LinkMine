The Handover

In 2002–04 China mined 85% of the world's reported antimony. In 2022–24 it mined 38%. Nickel, cobalt, uranium and phosphate rock saw swings just as large: in five minerals, one country's share of world output moved by 26 to 48 percentage points in twenty years.

Share of reported world mine output. Average of 2002–04, then of 2022–24.

Who digs what looks fixed. For a few minerals it is not. The British Geological Survey has recorded mine output by country for over fifty years, and when its 2002–04 figures are set against 2022–24, five of the twenty minerals that can be measured reliably show a single country's share swinging by more than a quarter of reported world output.

In three of the five, the leader changed. Russia led nickel in 2003; Indonesia leads it now. Canada led uranium; Kazakhstan does. The United States led phosphate rock; China does. In antimony and cobalt the leader stayed — China and DR Congo — but the margin did not: China's antimony lead shrank from 85% to 38% while Tajikistan climbed from 2.5% to 27%, and DR Congo took cobalt from a third of output to over 70%.

Read this before you quote a number

These are shares of the countries that report to BGS, not of a published world total — BGS does not publish one. Against the United States Geological Survey's 2025 country estimates, the same country ranks first in antimony, nickel, cobalt and phosphate rock, and the levels mostly agree: antimony China 37% (BGS 38%), cobalt DR Congo 75% (72%), phosphate rock China 45% (44%). The exception is nickel: USGS puts Indonesia at 72% against BGS's 55%, partly because Indonesia was still growing and BGS here is a 2022–24 average. Uranium cannot be cross-checked: the USGS data we hold has no matching series.

How this was built, and what it can't tell you

The source. BGS World Mineral Statistics, mine-stage production by country, 1970 to 2024. World shares are computed here as each country's output divided by the sum of countries reporting that mineral that year. Used with the permission of the British Geological Survey.

How the 20 minerals were chosen

  • 34 candidates: mine-stage production series with at least 20 years of record, at least 10 reporting countries and data to 2022 or later. Processed stages (smelter, refinery, alumina, steel) and fuels are excluded; this is a report about mining. Bauxite, iron ore and phosphate rock carry no stage label in BGS but are mined products, so they are included.
  • 4 dropped as low confidence: clays, barite, and two talc and pyrophyllite series, where BGS reporting is too heterogeneous for a share to mean much.
  • 10 failed the reporting-base test: a series needs at least 10 reporting countries in both periods, and at least 90% of 2022–24 output has to come from countries that also reported in 2002–04. Otherwise a share rises simply because more countries started reporting. Those that failed: beryllium, bismuth, manganese, platinum-group metals (two series), strontium, titanium minerals (two series), vanadium and zirconium.
  • 20 remain and are all shown in the table.

Coal was excluded on purpose

BGS records coal by grade, and in its grade series China's anthracite share falls from 82% to 0% and its bituminous share from 42% to 0%. That looks like a change in how China's coal is reported, not in mining, so no coal share is published here.

Would a different choice of years change the story?

Three-year averages are used at each end because single years are prone to reporting artefacts. The comparison was then repeated for four period choices: 2002–04 against 2022–24, 2005–07 against 2022–24, 2000–02 against 2020–22 and 2002–04 against 2019–21. The five minerals in the headline are the biggest mover in their own mineral under every one of the four, and each is among the seven biggest movers overall in every one of them. Phosphate rock is the weakest of the five: its swing is 26 points in the main comparison and as low as 19.5 in another. The table marks every mineral whose biggest mover changes with the choice of years; those rows are shown in grey and are not claims.

The two Congos

In our ingestion of the BGS data, some records for the Republic of the Congo were attached to DR Congo. The published layer excludes the affected records, and the DR Congo results here are corroborated: cobalt is 72% by BGS against 75% by USGS, and copper 13% against 16%. The charts start in 1995 because Kazakhstan's uranium has no separate record before 1991 (it was part of the USSR) and DR Congo's cobalt has none before 1991 in this data. A flat zero there would be missing data, not production of nothing, so it is not drawn.

Where the USGS comparison disagrees

For magnesium compounds the two sources disagree about Australia's share (13% BGS, 2% USGS), which may reflect a different definition. That mineral is also one whose biggest mover changes with the choice of years, so it is not used for any claim here.

Still true, and not claimed

A share of mine output is not ownership, profit or capacity, and this report does not say why shares moved. Context from outside the data: Indonesia's nickel share fell sharply in 2014–15 when it banned exports of unprocessed ore. That is public record, not something these figures prove. A small reporting base makes a share more fragile: antimony has 17 reporters now, so a country entering or leaving the list moves everyone's share even after the 90% test.
