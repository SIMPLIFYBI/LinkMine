Britain's North Sea peaked in 1999; it has imported oil since 2006 and gas since 2004. Indonesia was an OPEC member that quietly became a net oil importer in 2003. The Netherlands, the country that gave Europe its gas market, now produces under a third of what it burns. We tracked production against consumption for every country and both fuels over 36 years and dated every crossing of the self-sufficiency line. Twenty-six crossings went down. Twelve went up.

How this was measured

The measure. Self-sufficiency is production divided by consumption, as a percentage, for one country, one fuel and one year. Above 100% a country produces more of that fuel than it burns. It is not a measure of energy security or of trade balance: a country can produce more than it consumes and still import, and this says nothing about electricity, coal, nuclear or renewables.

What counts as a flip

A country–fuel pair qualifies only if it sustained a three-year average above 110% at some point and its latest three years average below 90% — or the reverse. Requiring a gap on both sides of the line keeps out series that merely hover at it. The crossing year is the first year after which the series never returns to the old side, so a one-year dip does not count as a flip. One pair, Papua New Guinea's oil, met the magnitude test but has no permanent crossing: it is still oscillating, and it is excluded from the counts.

"Peak" is the highest three-year average anywhere in the series, not the first observation. That distinction matters: Britain's oil self-sufficiency in 1990 was only 102%, and its real peak of 158% came in 1999. Measuring from the start of the record would have missed the textbook case entirely.

A publisher convention that had to be removed first

Where no trade is reported, the source sets consumption equal to production — to full floating-point precision. Those rows compute to exactly 100%, which reads as "perfectly balanced" but actually means "no trade data". Egypt's gas in 2000 is recorded as 18.3001727095257 produced and 18.3001727095257 consumed. Iraq's gas is identical in every year from 1995 to 2015.

This affects 431 of 2,513 gas rows, 17.2%, across 34 countries, and no oil rows at all — oil trade is always reported. Not one row in the data sits at exactly 100% without being identical, so every such reading is a placeholder rather than a genuine balance. Those rows are excluded here. Treating them as observations invents history: smoothing across the edge of Iraq's twenty-year placeholder run produces a 151% gas "export peak" that never happened, and on the uncorrected data Iraq appears in this report. It should not, and does not.

What the numbers do not control for

  • Petroleum production here is total supply of all liquids, including crude, condensate, natural gas liquids and refinery gain, while consumption is of refined products. A country can look self-sufficient in liquids overall and still import the specific fuels its vehicles burn.
  • Gas is an annual total in billion cubic metres; oil is a daily rate in thousand barrels per day. Percentages are comparable between the fuels; quantities are not.
  • The most recent year is a publisher estimate for an incomplete year and will move in later releases. Crossings dated 2024 and 2025 are the least settled.
  • A flip can be caused by production falling or by demand rising, and the two have very different meanings. China's oil crossing in 1993 was demand; Britain's in 2006 was depletion; Syria's and Yemen's were war; Sudan's was the secession of South Sudan in 2011, which took the oilfields and left the pipeline.
