Australia Nearly Caught Qatar on Gas

In 2013–15, Australia was producing about 40% as much natural gas as Qatar. By 2022–24, it was at 90%. Almost all of that catch-up happened in just a few years.

The two countries started remarkably close. In 2000, Australia produced about 33 billion cubic metres of dry gas, just ahead of Qatar’s 29. Qatar then pulled away. By 2013–15, it was producing roughly two and a half times as much gas as Australia.

Then came Australia’s turn. Queensland began exporting liquefied natural gas, or LNG, in 2015 after developing gas from coal seams. New export facilities opened during the decade. In four years, Australian dry gas output went from 72 billion cubic metres to 145. Qatar’s output stayed near the level it had already reached.

First Qatar pulled away. Then Australia closed most of the gap.

Australia caught up by growing faster

Australia added nearly 87 billion cubic metres a year between the two comparison periods. Qatar added about 7. Subtract one from the other and the gap narrowed by about 80 billion cubic metres a year. Qatar was still producing more at the end. Australia had grown much faster.

The annual figures show how compressed that change was. The gap fell from about 92 billion cubic metres in 2015 to 22 in 2019 and 16 in 2024. About nine tenths of the narrowing between 2015 and 2024 had already happened by 2019.

Only the United States, China and Iran added more annual output than Australia among the 162 countries with EIA figures for all six years. Qatar makes a useful benchmark for the catch-up, while the broader ranking shows how unusual Australia’s growth was.

The ten biggest gas producers

Australia moved into the same league as Qatar, but the next rung was still some distance away. Canada, in fifth place, produced about 47 billion cubic metres more than Australia in 2024, nearly three times the gap between Australia and Qatar.

Why all that gas doesn’t settle the local supply question

Geoscience Australia estimates that about 70% of the country’s gas production was exported in 2024, chiefly as LNG. That is a separate measure from the dry gas comparison above. It helps explain why a country can be a big producer and still face a different supply picture in each region.

Western Australia has no gas pipeline to the east coast. On the east coast itself, declining Bass Strait output changes where southern buyers can source their gas. How I measured it

I used the EIA’s annual dry natural gas production series as loaded in YouMine. The chart starts in 2000 to show both countries before the gap opened up. The headline compares the average of 2013–15, before the steepest part of Australia’s climb, with 2022–24, after it had levelled off.

I divided Australia’s average output by Qatar’s for each period and rounded after calculation. The production ranks use the most recent year shown in the chart, 2024.

I ranked the growth figures using the difference between the two three-year averages. A country needed a recorded figure in all six years to be included in that comparison. The 2024 production table ranks all countries with a figure in that year. The export share comes from Geoscience Australia’s national energy accounts.
