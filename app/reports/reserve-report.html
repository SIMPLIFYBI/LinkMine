Reserve Rich, Production Poor

Australia has the world’s biggest or second-biggest reserves of nine of the 29 minerals ranked here. It leads the world in mining just one of them: lithium. For the other eight, it ranks lower for what it mines than for what it holds in the ground.

The measure here is simple: how many years a country’s known reserves would last if it kept mining at today’s rate. A big number means a country is sitting on far more than it digs up. It isn’t a countdown to running out. Reserves grow as exploration finds more, and mining rates change.

Six minerals aren’t ranked, because their figures don’t line up (see the method): iron ore, bauxite, zirconium, titanium minerals, tantalum and vanadium. Australia is first or second for reserves of all six, and leads the world in mining iron ore and zirconium. So the headline applies to the 29 ranked minerals only.

    Handle with care: Australian nickel

    Australian nickel’s 556 years is the shakiest figure here. It uses estimated 2025 output of 45,000 t, less than half the 98,000 t mined in 2024. And only 11 Mt of the 25 Mt reserve figure meets Australia’s JORC reporting standard (or an equivalent). On that figure alone it is 244 years.

    How this was built, and what it can't tell you

    The measure. Reserves divided by one year of mine production. It compares what a country holds with how fast it is mining, and is not a forecast: reserves grow as exploration turns resources into reserves, and production rates change.

    Mine production only

    Refinery, smelter, plant and recycled output are excluded. China refines roughly 14 million tonnes of copper a year but mines 1.8 million; comparing refinery output with its own reserves would have put China at 2.9 years instead of 22.8.

    Totals removed

    Publisher totals and catch-all rows (“World total”, “Other countries”) are excluded, along with rows that combine several countries.

    The rankings have a floor

    The USGS tables list only countries above a size threshold and put the rest under “Other countries”. A rank of 9th of 9 means ninth among countries big enough to be listed on their own.

    Coal: only countries that mine it

    Coal ranks 63 countries. Seven more hold coal reserves but recorded no coal production in 2025 (Egypt, France, Italy, Morocco, Slovakia, Spain and Taiwan), so they have no years-of-reserves figure and are left out rather than shown with a production figure the data does not support.

    The latest year is an estimate

    The most recent year in each series is the publisher’s estimate for an incomplete year. Coal reserves are carried forward unchanged between editions rather than restated, so that series is a standing estimate.

    Six minerals not ranked

    Each is left out for its own reason. Zirconium reserves are counted as zirconium oxide while production is gross weight. Iron ore appears both as crude ore and as iron content. Titanium splits into ilmenite and rutile. None of these has a single like-for-like basis. Bauxite’s reserve and production lines are labelled differently in the source tables and are not yet confirmed comparable. Tantalum has reserves for only four countries, too few to rank. Vanadium has the unit problem below. On every basis USGS publishes, Australia is first or second for reserves of all six, and first for 2025 mine production of iron ore and zirconium.

    Why vanadium is excluded

    The source gives world vanadium reserves of 21,000 tonnes against 110,000 tonnes of annual production: less than one year. The reserve figures appear to be in thousands of tonnes while the unit column says tonnes. Until that is resolved, no vanadium figure is shown.

    Nickel

    Australia’s 556 years divides USGS’s 25 million tonne reserve figure by 45,000 tonnes of estimated 2025 mine output, less than half the 98,000 tonnes USGS gave for 2024. USGS also notes that only 11 million tonnes of Australia’s reserves are JORC-compliant or equivalent; on that figure the ratio is 244 years.
